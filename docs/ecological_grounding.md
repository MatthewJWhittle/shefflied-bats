# Ecological grounding for the South Yorkshire bats project

## Ecology review

**What this is.** A factual summary of the landscape ecology of the study area and the natural history of the bat species recorded there: where each species roosts, how it forages, which habitats it uses and its status in the region. Public sources only (listed in section 3).

---

## 1. The study area

The study area is the four South Yorkshire districts (Barnsley, Doncaster, Rotherham and Sheffield) plus a 7 km buffer. The buffer takes in edges of the Dark Peak, north-east Derbyshire, West Yorkshire and north Nottinghamshire. It crosses five Natural England National Character Areas (NCAs). Landscape descriptions below follow the NCA profiles (Natural England 2012a, 2012b, 2013a, 2013b, 2013c):

| NCA | Where | Landscape |
|---|---|---|
| 51 Dark Peak | West Sheffield and Barnsley | High gritstone moorland, blanket bog, heather, gritstone edges, reservoirs, conifer blocks |
| 37 Yorkshire Southern Pennine Fringe | Sheffield's western suburbs, Penistone, Stocksbridge | Steep wooded valleys (cloughs), pasture, gritstone villages, the Don, Loxley and Rivelin valleys |
| 38 Notts, Derbyshire and Yorkshire Coalfield | Sheffield and Rotherham core, Barnsley, the Dearne | Urban and industrial, former collieries, restored spoil, river corridors, mixed farming |
| 30 Southern Magnesian Limestone | A narrow north–south band east of Rotherham and through Doncaster's west side | Arable plateau, limestone gorges (for example Anston Stones, Roche Abbey), estate woodland, caves |
| 39 Humberhead Levels | East Doncaster | Flat, low-lying drained farmland, Thorne and Hatfield Moors (lowland raised bog), drains, the Don and Went, gravel pits |

### What each landscape means for bats

| Landscape | Roosting | Foraging and commuting | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Moorland, blanket bog and gritstone edges** (Dark Peak) | Almost none: no trees, few buildings. Some crevices in rock and walls | Poor: exposed and windy, cold, few insects | Altitude and exposure. Bat activity falls with height and wind in Pennine studies (Bellamy et al. 2013, Yorkshire Dales). |
| **Upland cloughs and wooded valleys** (Pennine Fringe) | Old oak and birch woods with tree holes; stone farm buildings and barns; old mills | Sheltered, insect-rich valley bottoms with streams; woodland edges | The best bat habitat in the west. A narrow band between moor and town. Used by woodland Myotis and brown long-eared |
| **River corridors** (Don, Rivelin, Loxley, Sheaf, Porter, Dearne, Rother) | Bridges, culverts, riverside trees and mills | Daubenton's over calm water; soprano pipistrelle along tree-lined banks; commuting routes into and through the city | Lighting and weirs break up continuity. Through central Sheffield and Rotherham the corridor is lit and canalised |
| **Reservoirs** (Damflask, Agden, Strines, Ladybower edge, Langsett, Ulley, Thrybergh) | Rarely roosts themselves. Nearby woodland and buildings | Large open water: Daubenton's, soprano, noctule; Nathusius' on passage | Upland reservoirs are cold and exposed. Water there is less productive than lowland lakes |
| **Urban core** (central Sheffield, Rotherham, Barnsley, Doncaster) | Buildings (pipistrelles), but dense cores have few suitable gaps | Low. Lit streets, little vegetation. Pipistrelles and noctules feed at some lights | Light-averse species (Myotis, long-eared) avoid it (Stone et al. 2009; Russo & Ancillotto 2015) |
| **Suburbs and parks** (the "green" Sheffield west and south-west) | Houses, churches, mature park and garden trees | Gardens, parks and tree-lined streets | Good for common pipistrelle; mixed for soprano; patchy for the rest |
| **Arable and pasture to the east** (Magnesian Limestone, coalfield farmland, Humberhead Levels) | Farm buildings, churches, hedgerow trees | Hedgerows, woodland edges and watercourses carry the bats; open fields away from edges are little used (Boughey et al. 2011a) | Intensive arable is insect-poor. Bats depend on linear features |
| **Magnesian limestone gorges and caves** | Caves and mines are used for hibernation (mainly Myotis and brown long-eared) | Gorge woodlands | Hibernation sites matter for population but aren't summer roosts. |
| **Post-industrial land** (spoil heaps, restored collieries, the Dearne valley wetlands like Old Moor) | Few roosts on the land itself. Nearby buildings | Wetlands and restored lakes in the Dearne are good foraging; bare spoil is poor | Restored land changes fast |
| **Lowland raised bog and wetland** (Thorne and Hatfield Moors) | Few: scrub and birch on the edges | Open, wet and insect-rich; noctule and pipistrelle feed over it | Remote, so few records |

## 2. Species: natural history in brief

UK status and distribution draw on the BCT species pages, the Mammal Society review (Mathews et al. 2018), the Mammal Society atlas (Crawley et al. 2020) and NBN Atlas maps.

**Common pipistrelle (*Pipistrellus pipistrellus*).** The commonest and most widespread UK bat, found across Yorkshire. Roosts almost entirely in buildings (houses, including modern ones). Forages along edges, gardens, parks, woodland and hedgerows. It tolerates the urban edge more than any other UK bat, and will feed around some street lights. Avoids high exposed ground.

**Soprano pipistrelle (*P. pygmaeus*).** Also widespread and common. Split from common pipistrelle only in the 1990s (Jones & van Parijs 1993), so older records are mixed. Roosts in buildings, often in larger colonies, usually near water. More tied to rivers, lakes and riparian woodland than common pipistrelle (Davidson-Watts & Jones 2006), and a little less urban-tolerant.

**Nathusius' pipistrelle (*P. nathusii*).** Rare, and a migrant. Many UK records are in autumn and winter, from animals coming over from the continent, but there are a few breeding sites. Strongly linked to large waterbodies, reservoirs and wetland. Roosts in buildings and trees near water.

**Noctule (*Nyctalus noctula*).** Widespread in England, though thinly. One of the largest UK bats. Roosts in trees: woodpecker holes and rot holes in mature trees, and rarely in buildings. Flies high and fast over open ground, water, woodland canopy and parkland, and travels several kilometres from roosts. Loud and easily detected on bat detectors.

**Leisler's bat (*N. leisleri*).** Scarce in Britain and patchy. Yorkshire is not at its range edge, but the species is thinly recorded there. Roosts mainly in tree holes, sometimes in buildings. Forages high in the open, like noctule. Calls overlap with noctule and serotine, so acoustic identification is uncertain.

**Daubenton's bat (*Myotis daubentonii*).** Widespread wherever there's calm water. Roosts in trees near water, bridges, tunnels and culverts. Feeds low over calm water, trawling insects from the surface. Avoids light: lit stretches of water are used less.

**Whiskered bat (*M. mystacinus*) and Brandt's bat (*M. brandtii*).** Both widespread but thinly recorded in northern England. The two are hard to tell apart in the hand and almost impossible from calls. Both roost in buildings and trees near woodland, and forage along woodland edges, hedgerows and riparian vegetation. Brandt's is more tied to woodland and water. Light-averse.

**Natterer's bat (*M. nattereri*).** Widespread but local. Roosts in old buildings (barns, churches), trees and bat boxes. Gleans insects in cluttered woodland and along tree-lined features. Strongly light-averse. Hibernates in caves and mines (possibly in the limestone gorges).

**Brown long-eared bat (*Plecotus auritus*).** Widespread and common in the UK, but often under-recorded by detectors because its calls are very quiet. Roosts in roof voids of old buildings (barns, churches, older houses) and in trees. Forages close to the roost, within 1–2 km, gleaning in woodland. Light-averse and a slow flier.
